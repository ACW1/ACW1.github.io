Gerrit Theodoor Rotman (1893-1944) was born in Groningen, but he was certainly no Groninger. The collapse of his father’s business put an end to any chance of putting down roots. The entire Groningen operation was liquidated, and within six months of the happy event mother and son were living with grandmother in Vlaardingen. Father had already left for Amsterdam a few months earlier.

Gerrit’s parents themselves were only passing through Groningen. They had arrived from Leeuwarden at the end of 1892, but had not lived there for long either. His mother, Neeltje van Toor (1870-1940), came from a family of entrepreneurs in Vlaardingen, while his father, Adriaan Jacob Rotman (1870-1941), came from Geesteren, near Borculo, in the Achterhoek. (Not to be confused with the Geesteren near Tubbergen in Overijssel.)

For Adriaan Jacob, Geesteren was merely the starting point of a picaresque tour of the Netherlands, in which the length of his stay in any given town was determined largely by the local success of his various commercial and financial ventures. His mobility stood in stark contrast to that of his forebears, for whom Geesteren remained the centre of the world, with distant Zutphen somewhere at its outer edge.

## Genealogy

G.Th. Rotman’s roots in the Achterhoek reach back into the seventeenth century, and probably even further.  
Adriaan Jacob Rotman, ‘financial agent’, born in Geesteren, was begotten by Arend Jan Rotman (1838-1872), headmaster, born in Zutphen.  
And Arend Jan Rotman was begotten by Engbert Rotman (1803-1882), court officer, born in Borculo.  
And Engbert Rotman was begotten by Arend Rotman (1774–1810), born in Geesteren, and Arend Rotman was begotten by Engbert Rotman (c. 1715-1793), born in Geesteren.  
And Engbert Rotman was begotten by Berend Ruiters (1671-1748), farmer, born in Eibergen.  
And Berend Ruiters was begotten by Willem Ruiters, whom we shall ignore from this point on, even though the direct male line continued through him. Instead, from Engbert onwards we switch to the female line.  
The aforementioned Engbert was begotten by Lutgert Rotman (c. 1680-1760), farmer, born in Geesteren.  
And Lutgert Rotman was begotten by Geert Rotman (c. 1650-before 1705), farmer, born in Geesteren.  
So all the generations from Gerrit to Geert are eight generations; and from Geert to the first Rotman in Geesteren there are x generations, for the time being still shrouded in mist.

## Farm name

Amid all this genealogical begetting, one rather striking thing was lost in Rotman’s direct male line – the surname Rotman. This is how it worked: until 1811, it was common in the eastern Netherlands to take one’s surname from the farm or holding where one lived or had been born. Since Geert Rotman’s offspring consisted of just two daughters, Lutgert and Jenneken, a male successor had to come from outside. Thus Lutgert Rotman remained Rotman after her marriage, while Berend Ruiters became a Rotman. Their children were given that surname too. (But when their son Engbert married Aaltje Bruntink and left the farm, he became Mr Bruntink.)

So there was a farm, an *erve Rotman*, near Geesteren. Strictly speaking, if we follow the male line, the farm from which Berend Rotman née Ruiters came could just as well be called the family’s cradle. But that one interests us rather less, simply because it does not bear the name of our comic-strip hero. Precisely because of that name, we want to know where the Rotman farm was located – or whether it is still there.

An important clue was the picturesque old church path between Borculo and Geesteren: the *Rotmansdiekske* ['the little Rotman dike']. According to the land registry, this lane has two addresses, both built in 1979. A little further on, along the Oude Diepenheimseweg, stands Versboerderij Daalwiek.   

A photograph showing a farmhouse, entitled *Rotman 1980*, suggests that a farm of that name still existed at least until that year. But where…? Had Rotman literally been wiped off the map? Might it still appear on maps from 1980 and earlier?

So, off to the Topotijdreis time machine of the Dutch Topographic Service!

Alas… the maps going back to 1815 do show farmhouses, but none of them is labelled Rotman. What can be seen, however – on maps from 1850 to 1889 – is the name *Rotmansbrug*. This little bridge in the Rotmansdiekske spared churchgoers the indignity of wading through the Kattenbeek in their Sunday best on their way to church in Geesteren or Borculo. The need for a bridge disappeared in the mid-1990s, when the Kattenbeek was disconnected at the Rotmansdiekske. A bridge symbol could still be seen on topographic maps well into the 2000s, but it has vanished in recent years. The area is too heavily wooded to establish via Google Maps whether anything of the Rotmansbrug still remains.

The word *De* at the upper right belongs to *Sprakelberg*, now the name and location of a cemetery near Geesteren.)

The name *Het Rot* implies that this branch of the Rotman family was not preceded by a man with an enormous red beard, but by a *rode* (rade, roede, roet, rot, troet): a piece of land made suitable for cultivation by clearing trees. When exactly the clearers set about turning the woods into a *rot* for Rotmans is uncertain. On a map from 1617 there was already no woodland visible at that spot. Earlier mapmakers were more interested in the local marshes and apparently considered the distinction between fields and woodland less important.

The so-called original cadastral register shows which plots belonged to a Rotman at the time, and which Rotman that was. The weaver Antoon Rotman, for example, owned a plot in Het Rot. And in Lemperhoek, north of Geesteren, there was a plot belonging to a Hendrik Rotman. ## From Geert to Gerrit

In the direct male line, we had not encountered another Gerrit after Gerrit Theodoor. Although Geert Rotman, the probable earlier inhabitant of the Rotman homestead, is also referred to somewhere as Gerrit. Besides the Engbert who became a Bruntink, Berend and Lutgert Rotman had three other sons: Willem (b. 1705), Jan (1709-1790) and Hendrik (1711-1751). Jan succeeded to the farm, possibly because Willem died young and Hendrik moved to ’t Loo.

The farmer Gerrit Rotman (1768-1851) was Jan’s grandson. In 1800 he married Essele Avink, who died in Lemperhoek in 1842. Their only child, Engele Rotman, died in Lemperhoek in 1842. Their granddaughter Garritdina died in Lemperhoek in 1842.

Poor Gerrit. Within a hundred days he lost three of those closest to him. What was going on in Lemperhoek? What were they doing there in the first place?

When Engele Rotman married Roelof te Winkel (1799-1858) from Ruurlo in 1822, the groom did not become Roelof Rotman. The couple did not settle at Rotman, but in the hamlet of Lemperhoek. Since he had not been born there, he may perhaps have acquired the use of a farm there, making it more logical to build their life there. Since his father-in-law Gerrit himself also died there in 1851, one wonders who was still living at Rotman after 1822...

## GRM|RTW 

What is certain is that the farm is now occupied by the Daalwijk family and that the former Rotman homestead is today called [Versboerderij Daalwiek](https://www.daalwiek.nl/). Although it lies closer to the Rotmansdiekske, its official address is on the Oude Diepenheimseweg. The photograph from 1980 will also have been taken from that road.

*GRM RTW 184[5]* probably commemorates alterations made to the farm in that year by the widowers Gerrit Rotman and Roelof te Winkel. Was this their way of dealing with their grief? In 1845 Gerrit was 77 years old and Roelof was 45 for most of the year. Who had they renovated Rotman for? The five surviving daughters and granddaughters eventually left home. Perhaps it was for the only son, Garrit Jan, who was five at the time.

How Rotman became Daalwiek I do not know. I only know that in 1896 a marital bond was forged between a Daalwijk (Maria Christina) and a Rotman (yet another Engbert). This Engbert descended through the line Jan Rotman → Berend Rotman → Teunis Rotman → Engbert Rotman → Willem Rotman → Engbert Rotman. The first in that sequence, Jan Rotman, appeared earlier as the owner of Rotman and the grandfather of the Gerrit Rotman who ran the farm around 1828. Teunis was therefore his brother, and the final Engbert his great-great-nephew.

So be it. Further clarification of the transformation from Rotman to Daalwiek is left as an exercise for the reader. It is time to say goodbye to the Rotmans of the Achterhoek. In part 2 of *The Rotman Timeline*, we shall move forward in time again and explain why G.Th. Rotman came into the world in Groningen.
